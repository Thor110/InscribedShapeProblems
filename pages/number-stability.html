Number Stability

Edward James Gordon — Odd Perfect Number Problem
Conjecture: the proper divisors of an odd number can never arrive exactly at N. No odd perfect number has ever been found, and none is known to be impossible: the question is open. An odd number lacks the N/2 divisor, so its largest proper divisor is at most N/3. That alone does not settle it: odd abundant numbers (the smallest is 945) pass N, and odd numbers come arbitrarily close to landing on it exactly. 1 · How many primes an odd perfect number needs

σ(N)/N = Π (1 + 1/p + … + 1/p^a) < Π p/(p − 1) over the distinct primes p of N, and p/(p − 1) is largest for the smallest primes. So an odd number with k distinct prime factors has σ(N)/N below the product over the first k odd primes. Perfection needs σ(N)/N = 2 exactly. This is the precise form of "no N/2 anchor": without the prime 2, odd numbers need several primes to get anywhere near 2.

2 · Odd numbers come arbitrarily close

The closest odd numbers up to 4,000,000, by σ(N)/N: none is perfect, but the misses get smaller. Then a construction that gets as close as you like: multiply distinct odd primes, each time choosing the smallest prime that keeps σ(N)/N below 2. The gap to 2 roughly squares at every step, so there is no fixed "gap" an odd number must leave, and no argument from the sizes of divisors alone can rule out an odd perfect number.

3 · Descartes' spoof

In 1638 Descartes found D = 3²·7²·11²·13²·22021 = 198,585,576,189. If 22021 were prime, the usual formula would give σ(D) = 2D exactly: an odd perfect number. It is not prime (22021 = 19²·61), and the true σ(D)/D is 2.1453. D satisfies every condition that uses only sizes and the multiplicative formula, so any proof that odd perfect numbers do not exist must use the fact that the factors are actually prime.

4 · Nearest misses: best undersized and best oversized

Every odd number up to ten billion, checked: how close does σ(N) come to 2N, from below (deficient) and from above (abundant)? Like the best undersized and best oversized approximations of the classical geometry problems, the two sides close in on the target from either side, and the target itself, a perfect odd number, lies between them. The dashed line is a miss of exactly 6: as a fraction of N the misses keep shrinking, but none lands on the mark, and apart from 3, 5 and 9 none comes closer than 6. The steps of 6 are provable for most of them: if N is odd, not a square, divisible by 3, and has a prime 5, 11, 17, … (two more than a multiple of 3) to an odd power, then σ(N) − 2N is a multiple of 6.
